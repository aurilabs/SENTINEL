# Research Question

## Working program question

Can a protective interface become a reliable material-state diagnostic system without sacrificing its primary barrier function?

The current first-stage question is narrower:

> Can a protective coating distinguish true corrosion initiation from benign coating damage and environmental confounders with a quantitatively calibrated, low-false-positive optical response?

## Why this question

Self-reporting and self-healing coatings already exist. Mechanical damage and corrosion have also been assigned different visual signals in prior work. The open problem is therefore not simply whether a coating can change color or fluoresce.

The practical question is whether the signal remains trustworthy when the coating is exposed to realistic environmental variation.

For a diagnostic interface, a useful signal should be evaluated against at least five requirements:

1. **Selectivity** — does the signal respond to the intended degradation state rather than unrelated environmental changes?
2. **Quantification** — can optical response be related to a physically meaningful severity metric?
3. **Independent validation** — is the optical result supported by electrochemical or other material-state ground truth?
4. **Uncertainty and false positives** — how often does the coating report corrosion when corrosion is absent?
5. **Protection penalty** — does adding diagnostic functionality reduce adhesion, barrier performance, or long-term durability?

## Initial application envelope

The first validation platform is carbon steel under marine/offshore-relevant conditions. This system is attractive because chloride exposure, humidity, wet/dry conditions and controlled coating defects can be studied at accessible laboratory scale while remaining relevant to infrastructure applications.

## Current hypotheses

- A single indicator channel is unlikely to provide robust diagnosis across realistic environmental conditions.
- Orthogonal channels based on different physical or chemical events may reduce ambiguity.
- Optical output should be calibrated to electrochemical ground truth rather than treated as a binary visual alarm.
- Environmental cross-sensitivity should be designed into the experiment from the beginning, not tested only after a sensor appears to work.

These are working hypotheses, not novelty claims.
